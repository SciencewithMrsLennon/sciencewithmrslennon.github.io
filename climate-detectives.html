📓 Field Notes

Every natural event in this lab changes climate by turning one of two knobs. Blocking sunlight means less of the Sun’s energy reaches Earth’s surface, so Earth cools. Trapping heat means less heat escapes back into space, so Earth warms. Remember: weather is what is happening right now. Climate is the average weather pattern over 30 or more years.

🌋 Volcano Watch

Erupt each volcano. Watch where the gas goes, then read the global temperature graph for the next 5 years.
Click an eruption to begin.

📓 Field Notes

A big eruption blasts sulfur dioxide gas into the stratosphere, high above the clouds. The gas forms tiny droplets called aerosols that reflect sunlight back to space. Rain can’t wash them out up there, so Earth stays cooler for about 1–3 years. When Mount Pinatubo erupted in 1991, Earth cooled about 1°F for about 2 years. In 1815, Mount Tambora caused 1816’s “Year Without a Summer.” Volcanoes also release carbon dioxide. Over millions of years, huge, long-lasting eruptions can trap heat and warm the climate.

📓 Field Notes

Small meteors burn up in the atmosphere. Only a huge impact changes global climate. It blasts dust and rock into the sky, and fires add soot. These block sunlight for months to years, causing an “impact winter.” Without sunlight, plants can’t do photosynthesis, so producers die first, then the animals that eat them. Evidence: the Chicxulub crater in Mexico formed 66 million years ago, and rocks around the world share a thin clay layer full of iridium, a metal common in space rocks. About 3 out of 4 species went extinct, including the dinosaurs.

🌊 Ocean Conveyor

The Gulf Stream carries warm water north to Europe. Near Greenland, the water cools, gets salty and dense, and sinks, which keeps the conveyor moving. Add fresh meltwater from melting glaciers and watch what happens.
None

🇪🇺 Western Europe: 50°F

📓 Field Notes

Ocean currents act like a conveyor belt that carries heat from the equator toward the poles. Cold, salty water is dense, so it sinks and keeps the conveyor moving. Fresh water is less salty and less dense, so it floats instead of sinking. A big flood of meltwater can slow or stop the conveyor, and the North Atlantic region can cool quickly, in just decades. Evidence: about 12,900 years ago, during the Younger Dryas, glacier meltwater poured into the North Atlantic and the region fell back to near ice-age cold. Layers in Greenland ice cores recorded it. Another ocean change, El Niño, spreads warm Pacific water east and makes some years warmer worldwide.

📓 Field Notes

Greenhouse gases like carbon dioxide, methane and water vapor trap heat like a blanket. Nature releases them through volcanic eruptions, wildfires, decomposition, respiration, thawing permafrost and warming oceans. Nature absorbs them through photosynthesis by plants and phytoplankton, oceans dissolving CO₂, and rocks and sediments that lock carbon away. When more is released than absorbed, Earth gets warmer. When more is absorbed than released, Earth gets cooler. Evidence: air bubbles in ice cores show that CO₂ and temperature have risen and fallen together for hundreds of thousands of years.
